Potato Chips in Mexico

North America

Mexico's chip story starts with Sabritas. Pedro Marcos Noriega started the business in Mexico City in 1943, making snacks at home and delivering them by bicycle; the name blends sabrosas (tasty) and fritas (fried). In 1966 the newly formed PepsiCo brought Sabritas into its portfolio on condition that it kept its Mexican name, and its first plant opened in Vallejo, in northern Mexico City, in 1968. Today the potato chip range runs from Original to Adobadas, Limón and Habanero, and the bags are a fixture of every tiendita (corner shop). The main rival is Barcel, Grupo Bimbo's snack arm, which began in 1977 with a factory in Querétaro and makes Chip's potato chips, but its biggest hit is Takis. Invented in 1999 and first sold as "Taquis", the rolled tortilla chips were renamed in 2004, and the purple-bagged Fuego (chilli and lime) arrived in 2006. Mexican snacking is built on lime, salt, chamoy, tamarind and chile, and chips are often prepared rather than eaten plain. In Mexico City, white aluminium-and-glass carts on plazas and street corners sell papas fritas drenched in hot sauce, Maggi seasoning and lime, while Tostilocos and Dorilocos turn a slit-open bag of Tostitos or Doritos into a meal with cucumber, jícama, cueritos (pickled pork rind), peanuts, chamoy and hot sauce.

Snacking Culture & Occasions

In Mexico, potato chips are most commonly enjoyed during street food accompaniment. Other popular snacking occasions include antojitos & casual meals, fiestas & family gatherings, chamoy-covered as a botana. The average bag of chips costs $1.20/bag, making them an accessible everyday snack across all demographics.

Did You Know?

Grupo Bimbo was once offered Sabritas and turned it down, co-founder Roberto Servitje has said. Its own snack arm, Barcel, later invented Takis.
